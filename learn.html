How it works

Nine small boards laid out as one big one. Everything follows from a single rule: the square you play in decides which board your opponent must play in next.

The one rule

Look at where a move lands inside its small board. Top right of that small board sends your opponent to the top right small board of the big one. Middle sends them to the middle. That is the whole game.

So every move is two decisions at once: what it does on the board you are in, and where it sends the other player next. A move that wins a small board and hands your opponent a dead one is worth more than either on its own.

Winning

  • Three in a row inside a small board wins that small board for you.
  • Three small boards in a row — across, down or corner to corner — wins the game.
  • A small board nobody wins counts for neither side.
  • If every board is settled and nobody has three in a row, the game is a draw.

The two rules people argue about

This site plays them the way they are set out below. They are the ones that differ from table to table, so it is worth knowing which you are playing.

  • A won board is still played in. If you are sent to a board somebody has already won, and it still has empty squares, you play there as normal. The result of that board never changes — it belongs to whoever won it first — but the square you take still decides where your opponent goes next. It is the sharpest rule in the game: a won board is a corridor you can steer people through.
  • Only a full board sets you free. You may play anywhere only when the board you were sent to has no empty squares at all. Not when it is won — when it is full.

Reading the moves

Moves are written the way chess writes squares: files a to i across, ranks 1 to 9 up. The whole big board is one nine by nine grid, so e5 is the middle square of the middle board and a9 is the far top left corner.

The move list beside the board uses it, the engine's lines use it, and you can read a game back from the list alone.

What to aim for

  • Think about where you are sending them, first. A good square in a board you are losing is usually worse than a poor square that sends your opponent somewhere useless.
  • The centre board is worth more. It is in four of the eight winning lines. So are the corners, in three.
  • Full boards are the free move. Sending your opponent to a full board hands them a free choice of the whole grid. Sending them to a board where every square is bad for them is the opposite.
  • Set up two threats at once. As in ordinary noughts and crosses, one threat is answered and two is a win — but here the threats are whole small boards.
  • Read the engine afterwards. When a game ends, the review opens: step through it and press Analyse the game to see where a position turned, and try your own lines from any move.

Playing with no connection

If a network blocks live play, the board has a by-message mode: every move produces a short code carrying the whole game. Send it however you already send things, and your opponent pastes it in.
